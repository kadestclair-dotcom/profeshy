## 0. Answer in one paragraph

House 113 (US, ruleset 1) publishes a new auction snapshot **once an hour in every hour observed, at a fixed minute: between 17 min 38 s and 19 min 32 s past the hour.** Across 21 observed changes, every gap between consecutive changes was 60–61 minutes, and every hour boundary that fell inside a probe run produced a change. Hours that were never observed (§6) are inferred to follow the same pattern, not measured. The API offers **no conditional requests** (no ETag, no Last-Modified, never a 304), so every check costs a full 10.0–14.6 MB download. The desk model's hourly assumption is therefore confirmed for the observed hours; its storage tables stand, and its 4.7 GB/day transfer estimate is superseded by the measured 4.5 GB/day (§3). A collector should fetch each house once an hour, shortly after the publish minute.

## 4. Collection design recommendation for the Contract (H2, H3)

**Schedule.** One fetch per house per hour at about :25 past the hour (about 5 minutes after the latest observed publish). If the fingerprint matches the previous snapshot, retry once at :40. If it still matches, record the hour as missed. This handles late publication without polling continuously.

**A failed request is not an unchanged snapshot.** No response, a 5xx or a 429 is a transport failure: it gets its own retry with backoff and is logged as a failure, never recorded as a missed hour or as "no change".

**Store only on change.** Fingerprint the auction-ID set exactly as the probe did; process and store only when it differs from the last stored snapshot.

**Volume at 15 houses (one ruleset), hourly.** Only house 113 was measured; these rows use its payloads as the estimate for all 15 houses. They are an extrapolation for capacity planning, not a measurement of 15 houses.

| Item | Per day | Per 30 days | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fetches | 360 (15 per hour) | 10,800 | 15 × 24 |
| Download | 3.6 – 5.3 GB (mean 4.5 GB) | 108 – 158 GB (mean 135 GB) | measured payload range × 360 |
| Blizzard rate limit headroom | 15 calls/hour against 36,000 calls/hour allowed | — | terms register §1, §2.17 |
| Storage growth, rollup R1 | — | ~90 MB/month after a ~0.5 GB working set | desk model C2 (unchanged) |

The always-on requirement remains the real D4 cost driver: a collector that misses an hour loses that snapshot for good, because the API never serves the past.

**Retention: rollup R1** (hourly kept 7 days, then daily per-item summaries). **This is conditional on compliance item C1**: Blizzard's terms §2.18 require a 30-day maximum retention on "all Data obtained through our APIs", and R1 keeps daily summaries indefinitely. Until C1 is resolved, R1 is the proposed design, not an adopted one. If C1 resolves against long retention, R1 is re-cut to a 30-day window and Stage 2's 90-day views and Stage 5 forecasting shrink accordingly.

**Product consequences (for Contract v1 and the UI):**

1. The finest possible grain is hourly; nothing between snapshots exists.
2. History starts the day collection starts. No backfill exists, and the app must say so.
3. Every price carries the time it was fetched (for example "fetched 10:25"). The publish time itself is known only to a window of about two minutes (:17:38–:19:32), so the app must not present the fetch time as an exact publish time. A price shown at 10:50 comes from the snapshot published between 10:17:38 and 10:19:32, up to about an hour old by design. That age should be shown, never hidden.
4. A missed hour is a gap and is drawn as a gap, never interpolated (charter §7.5).

## 5. Method note: how the publish window was derived

Each detected change is known only to within its 15-minute probe interval. Assuming the publish minute is the same every hour, the true minute must lie inside every one of the 21 change intervals, mapped onto the hour. Their intersection is :17:33 to :19:32. One no-change interval (18:02:36 → 18:17:38 on Nov 15) shows that hour's change had not happened by :17:38, which raises the lower edge to :17:38. Result: **:17:38 – :19:32**, 114 seconds wide. (Corrected after review #26: the first draft gave :17:36–:19:30; recomputed from the log, the intersection is :17:33–:19:32.) The assumption of a fixed minute is itself supported: no change ever fell outside this window, and no hour inside a run lacked one.

## 6. Limits of this evidence (read before relying on §2)

- **One house.** All of this is house 113, US, ruleset 1. Other houses may publish at different minutes. The collector must learn each house's publish minute from its own first day of data rather than assume :18.
- **Coverage gaps.** 22.3 hours over 4 days in 7 runs. Hours never observed (PST): 03–06, 13, 22 and 23. Hourly publishing in those hours is inferred from the pattern, not observed. A maintenance window or a slower overnight cadence would not be visible in this log.
- **Short span.** Four days, one week of the year, shortly after launch (WoW Forever launched 2026-11-04). Blizzard can change the cadence without notice (terms §1/§2.11, risk R1).
- **15-minute sampling.** A probe sees at most one change per 15-minute interval. It cannot rule out two changes inside one interval, or a change that reverted before the next probe.
- **Change detection uses the auction-ID set.** A snapshot that changed prices but kept exactly the same auction IDs would not register. Every detected change also changed the count, so this is unlikely, but the method cannot rule it out.
- **Run 7 is short** (30 minutes, no publish boundary crossed) and adds no cadence evidence; it is reported for completeness.
- **Design deviation.** The design specified one continuous 24-hour window. The evidence is 22.3 hours in seven runs. The author's view is that it answers H1 more strongly than one window would, because it spans four days and most hours of the day. **Owner decision 2026-11-18: accepted as satisfying the design's C1 cadence probe.** The unobserved hours listed above remain a stated limit.

All §2 figures are **Tested** (one method, one house). None is **Verified**: that would need a materially different method, for example a second house or the backend collector's own logs once it exists.
